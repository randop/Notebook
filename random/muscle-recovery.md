A few important clarifications on how to read those numbers:

**What "rest period" means here** — this is time between training sessions targeting the *same muscle group*, not total rest from exercise. You can and should train other muscles in between.

**Small vs. large muscles**

Small muscles (biceps, triceps, forearms, calves) have less total volume to repair. Large, multi-joint muscles (quads, back, chest, glutes) sustain more microtrauma and need the longer end of the range after heavy compound work (squats, deadlifts, rows, bench press).

**Why recovery lengthens with age**

The core driver is hormonal decline — testosterone and growth hormone both decline from the late 20s onward, reducing the rate of protein synthesis. Inflammation resolution also becomes slower, meaning soreness and cellular repair both take more calendar time. Satellite cells (the stem cells that repair muscle fibers) also become less responsive. None of this means older individuals should train less — just smarter.

**Universals that matter at every age**

- Sleep 7–9 hours. This is when GH is secreted and most repair happens. Skimping here negates all the rest-period math.
- Protein 1.6–2.2g/kg bodyweight daily, with older adults needing the higher end to overcome "anabolic resistance."
- Active recovery (light walking, swimming, mobility work) on rest days outperforms complete inactivity for clearing metabolic waste.
- Deload weeks every 4–8 weeks — reduce volume by ~40–50% to let connective tissue (which recovers slower than muscle) catch up.

**One caveat**: training intensity and volume matter more than age alone. A 55-year-old running moderate intensity with good sleep may recover faster than an undertrained 30-year-old on poor sleep. These ranges assume moderate-to-hard effort, adequate nutrition, and reasonable sleep.
