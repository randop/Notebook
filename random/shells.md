# Shells

**Seashells** (often just called "shells") are the hard, protective outer coverings of marine animals, most commonly found washed up on beaches or in shallow ocean waters.

### What Are Seashells Made Of?
- They are primarily composed of **calcium carbonate** (CaCO₃), the same mineral as limestone and chalk.
- The animal (usually a mollusk) secretes layers of this material over time to build its shell.
- Some shells also contain proteins and pigments that give them colors and patterns.

### Which Animals Make Them?
Most seashells come from **mollusks**, a large group of invertebrates that includes:
- **Gastropods** (single-shelled): Snails, conchs, whelks, periwinkles, cowries.
- **Bivalves** (two-shelled): Clams, mussels, oysters, scallops, cockles.
- **Cephalopods**: Nautiluses (their shells are chambered and float).

Other marine creatures like **barnacles**, **sea urchins** (their "tests"), and some **corals** can also leave hard structures that resemble or are collected as shells.

### How Do Shells End Up on the Beach?
1. The living animal uses the shell for protection from predators and the environment.
2. When the animal dies, the soft body decays or is eaten.
3. The empty shell is moved by waves, currents, and tides.
4. It eventually washes ashore, especially after storms, which dislodge many from the seafloor.

Over time, shells can break, erode, or get buried and turn into sediment that eventually forms limestone rock.

### Common Types You’ll Find on Beaches
- **Conch shells** — Large, spiral, often pink inside.
- **Scallop shells** — Fan-shaped with ridges.
- **Clam shells** — Various sizes, often hinged.
- **Sand dollars** — Flat, disk-shaped (actually sea urchin skeletons).
- **Olive shells**, **augers**, **murex**, **cowries** — depending on the region.

### Fun Facts
- Shells grow in layers as the animal gets bigger (they don’t shed their shells like crabs do).
- The beautiful colors often fade after the shell has been on the beach in sunlight for a while.
- Collecting shells is popular, but in many places it’s best to follow "take only photos, leave only footprints" — especially for live animals or protected species.
- Some cultures and traditions use shells as tools, jewelry, currency (e.g., cowrie shells historically), or in art.
